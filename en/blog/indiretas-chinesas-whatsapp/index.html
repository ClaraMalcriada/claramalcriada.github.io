7 Chinese Indirections That Fit in a WhatsApp Message

December 1, 2026 · 3 min read · by Clara Malcriada

The imperial court of Beijing and the family WhatsApp group have more in common than their headcount. In both, whoever talks too much loses. In both, information circulates in circles. And in both, whoever plays badly is handled with a courtesy so impeccable it takes days to hurt. The difference is that the imperial court, at least, deigned to justify its own rules.

For two thousand years the Chinese tradition of communicating discontent by indirection has produced exactly the material the West invented late, for the medium it invented late. Short proverbs, polite surface, terminal content. Seven of them — field-tested and approved by entire dynasties — fit in a single message:

  1. Playing the zither to a buffalo (对牛弹琴). For when you have explained something three times and the person replies with a question that was already answered. Send the proverb. The buffalo will spend a while trying to figure out what it means — which confirms the diagnosis.
  2. Lifting a rock to drop it on your own foot (搬起石头砸自己的脚). The standard reply to whoever criticized your work and then showed up with the same error signed beneath their own name.
  3. The blind men feeling the elephant (盲人摸象). For the meeting in which each participant drew a radical conclusion from a different corner of the problem and the three reports contradict each other.
  4. Water too clean holds no fish (水至清则无鱼). For the perfectionist colleague who returned the report with nine comments on the italics and zero on the content. Excessive rigidity drives people away; the proverb warns with the sweetness of an aquarium.
  5. The rumor dies in the ear of the wise (谣言止于智者). For the relative who forwards a scam chain message captioned "just checking this with you all". It closes the topic and the sender's standing in the same line.
  6. A knife hidden in the smile (笑里藏刀). The proverb here is an instruction for use, a message: it is the school of the "haha how great!!" with two exclamation marks, addressed to whoever has just announced in the group that your idea was wonderful — after trying to bury it in the hallway.
  7. The more words, the more mistakes (言多必失). For the ten-minute voice note. It works as a philosophical reminder for the other members of the group and as a sentence for the author.

The mechanism that makes all of this work in a group chat is the same that made it work in imperial correspondence: the offensive message is assembled by whoever receives it. You deliver first-rate historical material, the person translates it, and when the penny drops — sometimes days later, in the middle of dinner — the only witness is themselves. A screenshot proves what was written; it does not prove what was understood.

There is also a class advantage: answering with a two-thousand-year-old proverb repositions the argument on a different plane of gravity. The opponent argues; you cite. It is the difference between yelling at the market and asking the waiter for another bottle. Whoever is answered with a Chinese proverb understands, at once, that the conversation has changed register — and that the new register is higher and poorly lit.

It works with the mother-in-law, with the boss and with that relative who asks in the group when you will finally get a proper job. In these cases, the proverb is not sophistication: it is philanthropy.

Frequently asked questions

What is a Chinese indirection?

A criticism with a polite surface and a sharpened payload, in which the interlocutor assembles the offense and therefore suffers it. The Chinese tradition documented the practice two thousand years ago in proverbs, letters and court anecdotes.

How do I answer an irritating message without starting a fight?

With an indirect proverb, in the vein of "playing the zither to a buffalo" or "the rumor dies in the ear of the wise". The message stays polite, the point lands, and the exchange produces no case law in the group.
